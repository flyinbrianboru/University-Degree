Modh staidéirHow to study with these notes

Techniques that suit grammar with lots of small rules.

Recall before you revealName the triggerSpace it outMix topicsDrill the verb in sixesShadow the audioMake it about youKeep an error logUseful sites

Recall before you reveal

Read the question, say the full answer out loud in Irish, then tap. Getting it wrong first and then seeing the answer sticks far better than rereading the answer.

Name the trigger

For every mutation, say why before checking: "feminine after an", "preposition + article (Ulster: lenite)", "past copula", "genitive plural". The highlighted letters are there to train this.

Space it out

Revisit a topic after 1 day, 3 days, a week and a fortnight. Short daily sessions beat one long one.

Mix topics

Once a topic feels easy, do worksheets from two topics in one sitting (e.g. the past tense and the copula). Mixing forces you to choose the rule, which is what the exam does.

Drill the verb in sixes

Every verb topic follows the same six slots: statement, negative, question, negative question, "he says that…", "he says that… not". Dhún mé · Níor dhún mé · Ar dhún tú? · Nár dhún tú? · Dúirt sé gur dhún sé · Dúirt sé nár dhún sé. Run any new verb through all six aloud.

Shadow the audio

In Identification sentences play the recording, pause after each answer, repeat it with the same rhythm, then try producing it from the English alone.

Make it about you

Rewrite worksheet sentences about your own life, work and places. Is as Béal Feirste mé. Tá mé ar ollscoil le bliain.

Keep an error log

Note every correction from exams or tutors with the rule behind it. Review the log weekly; the boxes marked Exam-confirmed in these notes come from that kind of feedback.

Bhuail mé thú.I hit you.
Bhuail sé í.He hit her.
Bhuail sí é.She hit him.
Bhuail muid iad.We hit them.
Bhuail siad muid.They hit us.

WhyWhy this matters for the copula

The copula always uses the object set: Is múinteoir é (never sé), Is cairde iad (never siad). Know this table and half the copula is done.

Ulstermuid or sinn

muid is the everyday Ulster (and Connacht) form for "we/us"; sinn is the older and Munster form, also standard. Your notes use muid throughout.

Bí or is?

Irish has two verbs "to be". Bí (tá, the substantive verb) links a noun to an adjective, adverb, place or state. Is (the copula) links a noun to another noun: what something is, or who it is.

Bí (tá)Is (an chopail)
Tá mé mór. I am big.Is múinteoir mé. I am a teacher.
Tá mé go maith. I'm well.Is mise Brian. I'm Brian.
Tá an lá go deas. It's a nice day.Is dochtúir í. She's a doctor.
Tá post maith agam. I have a good job.Is le Diarmaid an carr. The car is Diarmaid's.

In general is describes what something permanently is; tá describes temporary qualities, where something is, or the state it's in.

TipQuick test

Is the word after "am / is / are" a noun (a teacher, the manager, Seán)? Use is. Is it an adjective, a place or a condition (tired, in Derry, at work)? Use tá.

UlsterTá sé ina mhúinteoir

You can also say what someone is with tá + i + possessive: Tá sé ina mhúinteoir (he is a teacher, lit. "he is in his teacher"). It's very common in Ulster speech. More on this in Other copula structures.

From the themes class

Tá + le: "have been … for"

Irish uses the present with le where English says "have been … for".

Tá mé ar ollscoil le seachtain (anuas).I've been at university for a week (now).
Tá mé anseo le bliain.I've been here for a year.
Tá sé anseo le mí.He's been here for a month.
Bhí mé ansin le seachtain.I had been there for a week.
Bhí mé ansin le coicís.I had been there for a fortnight.

NoteTwo things to notice

ar ollscoil, like ar scoil, has no article: it means attending, as English "at school" does. And Irish English "I'm here a year now" is this exact structure carried into English.

i or ar with place names

Use i (+ eclipsis) when the place name has no article. Use ar an when the name contains an.

Béal Feirste → i mBéal Feirstein Belfast
Gaillimh → i nGaillimhin Galway
Tá mé i mo chónaí i Lios na gCearrbhach.I live in Lisburn.
An Ómaigh → ar an Ómaighin Omagh
Tá mé i mo chónaí ar an Lorgain.I live in Lurgan.
Tá mé i mo chónaí ar an Chorr Chríochach.I live in Cookstown.

Ulsterar an with place names

Using ar with names that contain an is an Ulster habit (ar an Bhun Bheag, ar an Fhál Charrach); the Caighdeán would say san Ómaigh. Note ar an Chorr Chríochach: Ulster lenites after preposition + article where other dialects eclipse (ar an gCorr Chríochach). See Prepositions.

NoteWhat the names mean

Béal Feirste: mouth of the sandbank ford. Lios na gCearrbhach: fort of the gamblers. An Ómaigh: the virgin plain. An Lorgain: the long low ridge. An Chorr Chríochach: the round hill on the boundary. Doire: oak grove. An Srath Bán: the white river-meadow.

The pattern

Is + class (a noun) + who or what (pronoun or noun). The class is indefinite: a teacher, a nice person.

Is múinteoir mé.I'm a teacher.
Is mac léinn thú.You're a student.
Is cara é.He's a friend.
Is girseach í.She's a girl.
Is foghlaimeoirí muid.We're learners.
Is daoine deasa sibh.You're nice people.
Is Gaeilgeoirí iad.They're Irish speakers.
Is múinteoir (é) Seán.Seán is a teacher.
Is Gaeilgeoir (í) Máire.Máire is an Irish speaker.
Is cairde (iad) Seán agus Máire.Seán and Máire are friends.

WhyThe optional é, í, iad

The pronoun can anticipate a named subject: Is múinteoir é Seán is literally "he's a teacher, Seán". It's optional but must match the person: é for a man, í for a woman, iad for more than one.

Answers to An…? and Nach…?: Is ea (yes) / Ní hea (no).

WhyNothing mutates in the present

Is, ní, an, nach, gur change nothing: Ní múinteoir, An peileadóir, gur múinteoir. Ní only adds h before é, í, iad, ea and adjectives: Ní hea, Ní haoibhinn.

NoteClaoninsint: indirect speech

After deir sé, sílim, tá súil agam, cloisim (he says, I think, I hope, I hear), is becomes gur and ní becomes nach: Sílim gur Meiriceánach í (I think she's an American). Before a noun beginning with a vowel it stays gur: Dúirt sí gur Astrálach é.

UlsterIs ea → 'Sea

In speech is ea runs together as one syllable, 'sea, and you'll often see it written that way.

Answers in the past: Ba ea (yes) / Níorbh ea (no).

Consonants

Ba mhúinteoir mé.I was a teacher.
Níor mhúinteoir mé.I wasn't a teacher.
Ar mhúinteoir thú? Ba ea. / Níorbh ea.Were you a teacher? Yes. / No.
Nár mhúinteoir thú? Ba ea. / Níorbh ea.Weren't you a teacher? Yes. / No.
Dúirt sé gur mhúinteoir é.He said he was a teacher.
Dúirt sé nár mhúinteoir é.He said he wasn't a teacher.

Vowels

B'amadán é.He was a fool.
Níorbh amadán é.He wasn't a fool.
Arbh amadán é? / Nárbh amadán é?Was he a fool? / Wasn't he a fool?
Dúirt sé gurbh amadán é. / … nárbh amadán é.He said he was / wasn't a fool.

F + vowel: treat it as a vowel

B'fheirmeoir é.He was a farmer.
Níorbh fheirmeoir é.He wasn't a farmer.
Arbh fheirmeoir é? / Nárbh fheirmeoir é?Was he / wasn't he a farmer?
Dúirt sé gurbh fheirmeoir é. / … nárbh fheirmeoir é.He said he was / wasn't a farmer.

F + consonant: treat it as a consonant

Ba Fhrancach í.She was French.
Níor Fhrancach í.She wasn't French.
Ar Fhrancach í? / Nár Fhrancach í?Was she / wasn't she French?
Dúirt sí gur Fhrancach í. / … nár Fhrancach í.She said she was / wasn't French.

WhyWhy f behaves like a vowel

Lenited fh is silent. fheirmeoir sounds like "eirmeoir", so it takes the vowel forms (b', níorbh). In Fhrancach the first sound you hear is r, a consonant, so it takes ba, níor.

Watch outWhen the past looks like the present

l, n, r and vowels never show lenition, so Dúirt sé gur léachtóir é could be "is" or "was" a lecturer. Only dúirt v. deir, or the context, tells you (week 3 class note).

Classification or identification?

Classification puts someone in a class; the noun is indefinite (a teacher). Identification says who or which; the noun is definite: it has an / na, a possessive (mo, do, ár…), a name, or seo / sin.

Classification (indefinite)Identification (definite)
Is rúnaí mé. I'm a secretary.Is mise an rúnaí. I'm the secretary.
Is tógálaí é. He's a builder.Is é an tógálaí é. He's the builder.
Is bainisteoir í. She's a manager.Is í mo bhainisteoir í. She's my manager.
Is siopa é. It's a shop.Is é an siopa é. It's the shop.

The patterns

I / you / we / you (pl): Is + emphatic pronoun + definite noun.

Is mise an rúnaí.I'm the secretary.
Is tusa an t-altra.You're the nurse.
Is muidne na Gardaí.We're the Guards.
Is sibhse ár múinteoirí.You're our teachers.

He / she / it / they: Is é / í / iad + noun + pronoun again, or the emphatic form.

Is é an tógálaí é. / Is eisean an tógálaí.He's the builder.
Is í Máire an tiománaí bus.Máire is the bus driver.
Is iad na glantóirí iad. / Is iadsan na glantóirí.They're the cleaners.

This / that: Is é seo… / Is é sin…; the Is é is often dropped.

(Is é) sin an printéir.That's the printer.
(Is é) seo an banc.This is the bank.
(Is iad) sin na billí.Those are the bills.

Short answers

  • A pronoun follows an / nach → answer with the same plain pronoun. An í Aoife an duine is óige? Is í.
  • A noun comes between an / nach and the pronoun → answer Is ea / Ní hea. An meicneoir é Tomás? Is ea. An Sasanach í Bernie? Ní hea, is Albanach í.
  • A prepositional pronoun follows an / nach → repeat it, plain form. An agatsa atá mo pheann? Ní agam. Nach dósan a thugtar an t-airgead? Is dó.
  • An adjective follows an / nach → repeat the adjective. Nach deas an lá é? Is deas. Nach iontach an scéal é sin! Is iontach, go deimhin.
  • Emphatic forms (mise, agatsa, dósan, iadsan) never appear in the answer.

Why gender matters

Gender decides what an does to a noun, whether an adjective is lenited, and which pronoun (é or í) refers back to it.

| MasculineFeminine

Adjective after the nounno change: fear maith, crann glaslenition: bean mhaith, páirc ghlas
  • d, t, s (and l, n, r, which never show lenition) are not lenited after an: an duilleog, an tine.
  • sc, sm, sp, st never take t: an scoil, an spúnóg.

Watch outDictionary trap: f means masculine

English-language dictionaries mark m / f. Irish-language ones write f (firinscneach) = masculine and b (baininscneach) = feminine. Ó Dónaill (teanglann.ie) and the irregular-noun page of your noun list both work this way.

Reading gender from the ending

A first guess: a noun ending in a slender consonant (last vowel i: sráid, litir, traein) is probably feminine; one ending in a broad consonant (last vowel a, o, u: cnoc, rang, seisiún) is most likely masculine. Groups of meaning

  • Countries are mostly feminine: an Fhrainc, an Iodáil, an Ghearmáin, an Astráil. Masculine: Sasana, Meiriceá, Ceanada.
  • Languages are feminine: an Ghaeilge, an Fhraincis, an tSínis. Exception: an Béarla (m).
  • Rivers are mostly feminine: an tSionainn, an Éirne, an Bhóinn. Exception: an Feabhal (the Foyle, m).
  • Body parts: outer ones mostly feminine (an chluas, an tsúil, an chos), inner ones mostly masculine (an croí, an béal, an t-ae, an duán).
  • Female people are feminine (an bhean, an mháthair, an bhanríon), but cailín is masculine: the -ín ending wins.

Weak plurals (lagiolraí)

A plural is weak if it's made in one of two ways:

  • (a) the final broad consonant is slenderised: fear → fir, amhrán → amhráin, peann → pinn, bád → báid, páipéar → páipéir, cléireach → cléirigh, curadh → curaidh.
  • (b) -a is added with no other change: bróg → bróga, úll → úlla, cos → cosa, cearc → cearca, leabharlann → leabharlanna, baintreach → baintreacha.

Group (a) is mostly 1st declension, group (b) mostly 2nd declension.

Strong plurals (tréaniolraí)

Every other plural is strong, and strong plurals come in all five declensions.

Gender stops mattering in the plural. What matters is how the plural noun ends: a slender consonant lenites the adjective.

Strong and weak plurals in the genitive

  • Strong plural: the adjective keeps the same plural form in every case: ag cur na gceisteanna suimiúla.
  • Weak plural: the genitive plural adjective looks like the nominative singular: ag moladh na bhfear suimiúil.
  • Never lenite in the genitive plural, even after a feminine noun: na mban cliste, na gceisteanna casta.

Exam-confirmedGenitive plural

Weak genitive plural adjectives stay completely unchanged. With strong plurals, 1st-declension adjectives take the plural form (-a); 3rd-declension (vowel) adjectives don't change.

Intensifying prefixes

  • an- (very) lenites, except d, t, s: an-mhaith, an-deas, an-te.
  • ró- (too) lenites: róthe (too hot), ródheas (too nice). Níl sé ródheas: it's not great.

The exceptions

  • ar doesn't lenite in set phrases of position, state or time: ar bord (on board), ar talamh, ar muir, ar bogadh (moving), ar buile (mad), ar ceal (cancelled), ar crith (shaking), ar crochadh (hanging), ar deireadh (behind), ar díol (for sale), ar fáil (available), ar fónamh (in good order), ar meisce (drunk), ar mire (crazy), ar maidin, ar ball (later). It does lenite for a real location or something "on" a person: tá cóta ar Sheán, tá áthas ar Shíle.
  • thar doesn't lenite in adverbial phrases: thar barr (great), thar ceal (overdue), thar ceann (on behalf of), thar fóir (over the top), thar sáile (abroad).
  • gan doesn't lenite d, f, s, t (gan Dónall, gan fáth, gan teach, gan slacht), a person's name (gan Micheál, gan Caoimhín), a noun with something attached (gan blas ar bith, gan clú maith, gan pingin rua) or a verbal noun (gan corraí, gan moladh a thabhairt). It does lenite a bare noun: gan mhaith (useless), gan chiall (senseless).
  • um doesn't lenite b or p: um bóithre (regarding roads), um pobail.
  • idir lenites only when it means both … and: idir fhir agus mhná (both men and women), idir bheag agus mhór. Meaning between, no change: idir Baile Átha Cliath agus Béal Feirste, idir meán oíche agus meán lae.

The root is everything

Every regular verb is built from its root (fréamh an bhriathair), which is also the order to one person: dún (close!), bris (break!), ceannaigh (buy!). Learn the root and the group, and every tense follows a pattern.

  • Regular verbs follow set patterns. They split into short verbs (1st conjugation, mostly one syllable) and long verbs (2nd conjugation, mostly two syllables).
  • Irregular verbs: only eleven, but they're the most common verbs in the language, so learn them by heart: abair, beir, bí, cluin / clois, déan, faigh, feic, ith, tabhair, tar, téigh. See The irregular verbs.

What it does

1. A noun

Tá an scríobh sin go deas.That writing is nice.
Tá an damhsa go maith duit.Dancing is good for you.
Tá an tógáil tosaithe.The building has started.
Tá an t-ól go holc duit.Drinking is bad for you.
Is cuidiú é.It's a help.

2. "To …"

Ba mhaith liom cártaí a imirt.I want to play cards.
Is breá liom seacláid a ithe.I love to eat chocolate.
Tá mise sásta sin a dhéanamh.I'm happy to do that.
Caithfidh mé fanacht.I have to wait.
Ba mhaith liom labhairt léi.I want to speak to her.
Dúirt sé liom imeacht.He told me to leave.
D'iarr sé orm íoc as an bhéile.He asked me to pay for the meal.

WhyWhen do you need "a"?

With a direct object, the order is object + a + verbal noun, and a lenites: cártaí a imirt, sin a dhéanamh. No object, no a: Caithfidh mé fanacht. An object that comes through a preposition isn't direct, so still no a: labhairt léi, íoc as an bhéile.

3. "-ing", with ag

Tá Seán ag tógáil tithe.Seán is building houses.
Bhí an cailín ag damhsa.The girl was dancing.
Tá an teagascóir ag caint.The tutor is talking.
Tá na páistí ag amaidí.The children are messing.
Thosaigh siad ag obair.They started working.

The verbal noun has no tense; the main verb carries it.

The object of ag + verbal noun is genitive

Tá sé ag dúnadh an dorais.He's closing the door. (doras → an dorais)
Tá sé ag glanadh na fuinneoige.He's cleaning the window. (fuinneog → na fuinneoige)
Tá sí ag déanamh obair mhaith.She's doing good work.
Tá sí ag ithe dinnéar blasta.She's eating a tasty dinner.

WhyWhy no genitive in the last two?

With no article and an adjective, the object stays nominative: obair mhaith, not oibre maithe.

A pronoun object goes in front

Tá sé do mo bhualadh.He's hitting me.
Tá sé do do bhualadh.He's hitting you.
Tá sé á bhualadh.He's hitting him.
Tá sé á bualadh.He's hitting her.
Tá sé dár mbualadh.He's hitting us.
Tá sé do bhur mbualadh.He's hitting you (pl).
Tá sé á mbualadh.He's hitting them.

Whyá = do + a

á is do + a (his, her, their) run together; the fada marks the lengthening and keeps it apart from the relative a. The mutation tells you who: á bhualadh (him), á bualadh (her), á mbualadh (them).

Mutation rules worth memorising

  • Past: never lenited, and no d', even after níor, ar, nár: Óladh an bainne, Níor moladh dom dul, Ar gortaíodh mórán daoine? The irregular bhíothas, chonacthas, chualathas, chuathas, thángthas keep their lenition.
  • Present and future: unchanged on their own, but particles mutate as usual: Ní chaitear, Ní chaithfear, nach mbainfear.
  • Past habitual and conditional: lenited, with d' before a vowel or f: dhéanfaí, bhristí, d'fhoghlaimítí, d'íosfaí.

An clásal coibhneastaThe relative clause

From: An Clásal Coibhneasta: nótaí Béarla; Clásal Coibhneasta: bileog oibre (+ freagraí)

What it doesThe direct relative (an clásal coibhneasta díreach)The indirect relative (an clásal coibhneasta indíreach)Worksheet: the relative clause

What it does

A relative clause adds information about someone or something without starting a new sentence: I saw a man. He was working here yesterday. → I saw the man who was working here yesterday. Irish has two kinds, and choosing between them is the whole skill.

The direct relative (an clásal coibhneasta díreach)

Use it when the person or thing is the subject or the object of the verb in the clause.

An gasúr a bhris na rialacha.The boy who broke the rules. (subject)
An cailín a thiocfaidh anseo amárach.The girl who will come here tomorrow.
An sagart a theagascann sa scoil.The priest who teaches in the school.
An teach a cheannaigh an fear.The house which the man bought. (object)
An duine a fheicim gach maidin.The person who I see every morning.
An t-airgead a chaithfidh mé ar saoire.The money which I'll spend on holiday.

The particle a + lenition

a is used in all tenses and lenites the verb, with these exceptions:

tá → atáan fear atá anseothe man who is here

abair, every tensean bhean a dúirt sin; an bráthair a déarfaidh an léachtthe woman who said that; the brother who'll give the lecture

The past autonomous of irregular verbs keeps its lenition: bhíothas, thángthas, chuathas, chualathas, chonacthas.

Negative: nach (+ eclipsis) and nár (+ lenition)

  • nach + eclipsis in all tenses: an múinteoir nach dtagann ar scoil gach lá (the teacher who doesn't come to school every day); an bhean nach n-ólann fíon. In the past, only with the irregular verbs bí, déan, faigh, abair, feic, téigh: an leanbh nach bhfuair an duais.
  • nár + lenition in the past with regular verbs and beir, clois / cluin, ith, tar: na scoláirí nár bhris na rialacha; sin an buachaill nár oscail a bhronntanas.

Other places the direct a appears

  • After cá mhéad, cá fhad, cathain, cén uair, conas: Cá mhéad duine a tháinig anseo aréir? Cá fhad a chaithfidh tú sa Fhrainc?
  • After nuair and mar: Nuair a bhaineann Dún na nGall craobh na hÉireann arís, beidh mé sásta. Bhí sé ina sheasamh ag an doras mar a bheadh saighdiúir ann.

The indirect relative (an clásal coibhneasta indíreach)

Use it when the person or thing is not the subject or object: whose, with whom, in which, to whom. A possessive (a mhac) or a prepositional pronoun (leis, ann, dó) later in the clause points back to it.

An fear a bhfuil a mhac tinn.The man whose son is sick.
An bhean a ndeachaigh a mac abhaile go luath.The woman whose son went home early.
An gluaisteán a bhfuil a úinéir taobh amuigh.The car whose owner is outside.
An múinteoir a raibh mé ag caint leis.The teacher I was talking to.
An cófra a bhfuil na cupáin ann.The press the cups are in.
An seomra a mbeidh na páistí ann.The room the children will be in.
An duine a dtugaim cuidiú dó.The person I give help to.

The particle a + eclipsis (dependent form)

  • a + eclipsis in all tenses: an bhean a raibh a mac anseo; an fhoireann a mbainfidh a captaen imreoir na bliana (the team whose captain will win player of the year).
  • In the past, regular verbs use ar + lenition instead, and drop d': an gasúr ar bhris a chara an fhuinneog (the boy whose friend broke the window); an leabhar ar bhain mé an clúdach de (the book I took the cover off); sin an bhean ar ól a cara do ghloine fíona.
  • The six irregulars bí, déan, faigh, abair, feic, téigh keep a + eclipsis in the past: a raibh, a ndearna, a bhfuair, a ndúirt, a bhfaca, a ndeachaigh.
  • Negative: nach + eclipsis (an múinteoir nach dtagann a mhac ar scoil); past with regular verbs nár + lenition (na scoláirí nár bhris a gcairde na rialacha).

Exam-confirmedtabhair in the indirect past

The notes group tabhair with the ar / nár verbs (ar thug). In Ulster it behaves like the six irregulars: a dtug, nach dtug; your exam feedback marked a dtug as correct. Also confirmed: ar chasadh (lenition after ar).

Where the indirect relative is required

  • After cé, cad / céard + a preposition: Cé leis a raibh tú ag caint? (who were you talking to?); Cé dó a dtabharfaidh tú an t-airgead?; Céard faoi a mbíonn siad ag gearán?
  • After an áit / cén áit, an dóigh / cén dóigh, an fáth / cén fáth, cad chuige: Sin an áit a ndearna sé an damáiste. Cén dóigh a ndéanann siad anseo é? Cén fáth ar tháinig sé ar ais sa deireadh?
  • After time words (lá, am, oíche, bliain) either is fine: Cén lá a bhí sé ann? / Cén lá a raibh sé ann?; Cén bhliain a tharla sé? / Cén bhliain ar tharla sé?

TipThe pointer test

After drafting the clause, look for a word that points back: a (his/her/their), leis, ann, air, dó, de… If there is one, the relative is indirect. If the noun is simply the doer or the thing done, it's direct.

TipThe six odd ones in the past

bí, déan, faigh, feic, téigh, abair take ní / an / go / nach in the past. The other five (beir, cluin, ith, tabhair, tar) take níor / ar / gur / nár, like regular verbs. Say the six as a chant: bí, déan, faigh, feic, téigh, abair.
